\section{Experimental Results}


We use \textsc{RippleEval} to study how a localized factual update affects connected non-target knowledge. 
Our experiments are organized around six research questions:

\textbf{RQ1: }Do factual updates produce non-local changes in connected knowledge?

\textbf{RQ2: }Are affected facts equally sensitive to these changes?

\textbf{RQ3: }Can corrupted non-target knowledge further propagate errors?

\textbf{RQ4: }Can knowledge similarity explain the observed ripple pattern?


\textbf{RQ5: }Can the observed propagation pattern guide more effective preservation?


\textbf{RQ6: }What internal changes are associated with the observed ripple pattern?

\subsection{Undesired Ripples Exists in Long Distance}
\label{subsec:generalization}

We begin with the first question: whether the side effects of a factual update remain local or the effect persists across multiple related hops.
To test this, we use the correct-to-wrong flip rate of knowledge to measure error propagation over non-target facts at hop distances $d=1,\ldots,5$ from the edited fact. 
For each distance, we retain only facts that the model answered correctly before the update, so that evaluation captures update-induced correct-to-wrong changes rather than pre-existing model errors. 

Figure~\ref{fig:blast_radius} shows that factual updates produce clear non-local ripple effects. 
Error propagation remains measurable across multiple hops, and in several models the distortion persists even at $d=5$. 
This indicates that a single localized update can affect facts that are several relational steps away from the target, suggesting that update-induced errors can travel through connected factual knowledge rather than stopping at the edited fact.

\subsection{Popular Knowledge Is More Vulnerable to Updates}
\label{subsec:popular_vulnerability}

Having shown that factual updates can produce long-range ripple effects, we next ask whether all connected facts are equally affected.
A natural hypothesis is that long-tail knowledge could be more fragile, as prior work has shown that models are more prone to hallucination or factual errors on less frequent knowledge \yuji{cite}.
We therefore examine this question by grouping the evaluated facts by the in-degree of their grounded object entity, following the factual popularity definition in Section~\ref{sec:dataset}.
High-Popularity facts correspond to widely shared answer entities, while Low-Popularity facts correspond to sparsely connected answer entities.


\paragraph{Vulnerability as direct updated knowledge.}
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:popular_vulnerability}, counter-intuitively, High-Popularity facts are more likely to flip from correct to incorrect after a nearby update than Low-Popularity facts.
Pooling neighboring facts across hops $d{=}1$--$d{=}5$, High-Popularity facts consistently exhibit a higher Flip Rate than Low-Popularity facts in all four evaluated models, with gaps ranging from $+3.4$ to $+11.6$ percentage points (largest on Qwen3.6-27B: $42.2\%$ vs.\ $30.7\%$).
This indicates that facts grounded in highly connected answer entities are not necessarily more stable; instead, they are more easily overturned when nearby factual knowledge is modified.

\paragraph{Vulnerability as neighbor knowledge.}
We further test whether this vulnerability persists when popular facts are not the edited source, but only neighboring facts affected by the update. 
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:innocent_bystander}, High-Popularity neighbors experience a larger accuracy drop regardless of the source type. 
Notably, even when the update targets a Low-Popularity source, High-Popularity neighbors still flip at $39.7\%$, compared with $35.0\%$ for Low-Popularity neighbors, a $+4.7$ percentage-point difference.
This result suggests that popular knowledge is especially sensitive to collateral changes: it can be affected not only when it is directly involved in an update, but also when updates occur in its factual neighborhood.

\subsection{Popular Knowledge Causes Wider Error Propagation}
\label{subsec:popular_propagation}



We next examine whether factual popularity affects the impact of an update when the popular fact is used as the update source. While the previous section shows that High-Popularity facts are more vulnerable as affected neighbors, this section asks whether updating a High-Popularity fact also causes wider downstream damage.

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:propagation_depth}, updates targeting Popular facts generally produce stronger downstream ripple effects, with the clearest and most consistent pattern on Qwen3.5-9B. The other models show noisier hop-level variation, but the overall pattern still suggests that Popular facts can act as stronger propagators of update-induced corruption.

\subsection{Surface Similarity Does Not Explain Most Ripple Effects}
\label{subsec:semantic_control}

A natural alternative explanation is that the observed ripple effects are caused by surface-level confusion rather than factual connectivity. For example, if an update about \textit{Apple Inc.} causes a flip in a fact about \textit{Apple Corps}, the error may arise because the entity names are similar, not because the two facts are connected through factual relations. To examine this possibility, we measure the normalized string similarity between the edited source entity and the affected neighbor entity using the Levenshtein ratio, and conduct two complementary analyses.

\paragraph{Broad similarity analysis.}
We first analyze approximately 139,000 source-neighbor pairs pooled across the four evaluated models, retaining only facts answered correctly before the update. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:broad_similarity}, entity pairs with very high string similarity ($\geq 0.8$) do have a higher Flip Rate, around 63\%. However, such pairs are rare and account for less than 1\% of all evaluated pairs. In contrast, low- and moderate-similarity pairs ($<0.4$) account for about 90\% of evaluated pairs and nearly 89\% of all flips. The overall Pearson correlation between string similarity and binary flip status is also weak ($r=0.05$). These results suggest that surface similarity can increase risk in a small subset of cases, but it does not explain the majority of observed flips.

\paragraph{Paired control within the same factual neighborhood.}
We further conduct a stricter paired analysis by comparing neighbors from the same update source and the same hop distance. This controls for the edited fact and the distance from the update, allowing us to test whether more similar entities are more likely to flip within the same factual neighborhood.

Under this paired setting, string similarity is not a reliable predictor of factual flips. The mean within-group Pearson correlation between similarity and flip status is essentially zero across 588 (source, hop) groups in the pre-update-correct pool ($r \approx 0.00$, median $r \approx 0.006$), with positively and negatively correlated groups approximately balanced (313 vs.\ 275). The same pattern holds within each individual model (see Appendix~\ref{app:semantic_diagnostics}, Table~\ref{tab:paired_r_per_model}). Neighbors with similarity $\geq 0.5$ are also extremely sparse in the paired pool, accounting for approximately 3\% of paired samples.

Together, these results show that surface similarity is not the main driver of the ripple effects we observe. Although similar entity names can introduce local confusion, the broad pattern of update-induced flips cannot be reduced to near-duplicate entity names. This supports our interpretation that factual connectivity, rather than question wording or entity-name similarity alone, is necessary for understanding how update effects spread across related knowledge.

\subsection{Popularity Anchoring Mitigates Ripple Effects}
\label{subsec:mitigation}

The previous sections show that high-popularity facts are both more vulnerable to neighboring updates and more influential when used as update sources. This raises a practical question: can high-popularity facts also be used to stabilize knowledge updating? To answer this question, we test a mitigation strategy called \textbf{Popular Anchoring}. The key idea is to preserve the model's behavior on a small set of high-popularity factual anchors while updating the target fact.

During the update, we optimize the model with two objectives. The first objective is the standard cross-entropy loss $\mathcal{L}_{CE}$ for learning the injected fact. The second objective is a KL-divergence regularizer that keeps the edited model close to the original model on a small set of anchor prompts $\mathcal{A}$:
\begin{equation}
\small
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}
&= \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}} + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{anchor}}, \\
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{anchor}}
&= \sum_{x \in \mathcal{A}}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(
P_{\mathrm{clean}}(\cdot \mid x)
\,\|\, 
P_{\mathrm{edited}}(\cdot \mid x)
\right).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $P_{clean}$ is the output distribution of the original model, $P_{edited}$ is the output distribution of the edited model, and $\lambda$ controls the regularization strength. We set $\lambda=0.1$ in all experiments.

For \textbf{Popular Anchoring}, the anchor set $\mathcal{A}$ consists of QA prompts whose answer entities have high factual popularity, measured by object in-degree. To prevent test-set leakage, anchor prompts are drawn from factual regions that share no paths with the target update neighborhood. We compare Popular Anchoring against three contrasts that hold all training hyperparameters fixed and vary only the anchor pool: \textbf{No Anchoring} (no KL regularizer), \textbf{Average Anchoring} (anchors sampled uniformly from the non-hub pool, so anchor popularity tracks the pool mean), and \textbf{Rare Anchoring} (anchors deterministically drawn from the lowest in-degree stratum of the same non-hub pool).


\input{tables/mitigation_results}

\paragraph{Comparison across the popularity spectrum.}
Table~\ref{tab:mitigation_results} reports the per-hop Flip Rate (Eq.~\ref{eq:flip_rate}) after updating under each anchor strategy at $N{=}100$. Popular Anchoring attains the lowest Flip Rate at every hop except $d{=}3$, lowering the average from $80.7\%$ (No Anchoring) to $68.8\%$ --- a $6.5$ pt gap over Average Anchoring and a $4.0$ pt gap over Rare Anchoring. Rare Anchoring is markedly weaker than Average Anchoring in the immediate neighborhood ($d{=}1$: $92.5\%$ vs $82.0\%$), indicating that low-popularity anchors fail to protect facts close to the update site; their benefit only emerges at $d{\geq}2$. These results suggest that high-popularity anchors are the most effective stabilizing constraint across the entire ripple radius.


\paragraph{Sample efficiency.}
We further vary the number of anchor prompts $N$. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:mitigation_efficiency}, Popular Anchoring already cuts the average Flip Rate from $79.8\%$ (No Anchoring baseline) to $70.9\%$ with only $N{=}5$ anchors, and continues to improve to $68.0\%$ at $N{=}100$. In contrast, Average Anchoring oscillates in $71\%$--$75\%$ and Rare Anchoring stays near $72\%$ regardless of $N$. The resulting $6.7$ pt gap between Popular and Average Anchoring at $N{=}100$ suggests that selecting anchors by factual popularity is more efficient than uniform random sampling, particularly when the anchor budget is small. 

A per-source-group breakdown in Table~\ref{tab:mitigation_by_source} 
shows that Popular Anchoring attains the lowest average within every source 
group (Popular / Average / Rare), confirming that the pooled advantage is 
not driven by a single update tier.

\subsection{External Validation under Batched Updates}
\label{subsec:external_batched_validation}

We next test whether popularity-aware preservation remains useful when
updates come from external benchmarks and are learned in batches rather
than one fact at a time.  We construct three entity-disjoint batches of
25 updates from each benchmark and train one LoRA adapter per batch.  We
compare an update-only \textbf{Baseline} with rehearsal on 100
\textbf{Popular}, \textbf{Average}, or \textbf{Rare} facts.  All
conditions use the same frozen rehearsal sets and training protocol.
For WikiBigEdit and WikiFactDiff, Table~\ref{tab:external_batched}
reports the Rare stratum of the frozen graph holdout, where answer
entities have low in-degree.  For CounterFact, it reports the
benchmark's native neighborhood locality.  Each entry is averaged over
three batches and two random seeds.

On WikiBigEdit, all rehearsal strategies substantially reduce
forgetting relative to the update-only baseline.  Popular rehearsal
achieves the lowest Flip Rate on the Rare holdout stratum reported in
Table~\ref{tab:external_batched}, reducing it from $58.7\%$ to $18.2\%$,
outperforming Average and Rare rehearsal by $2.8$ and $6.4$ points,
respectively.  This supports Popular rehearsal as the strongest
strategy for preserving low-popularity holdout knowledge in this
setting.  WikiFactDiff shows the same ordering: Popular rehearsal
reduces Flip Rate from $26.1\%$ to $14.9\%$, outperforming Average and
Rare rehearsal by $3.0$ and $8.6$ points, respectively.  Popular beats
Average in five of six paired batch--seed runs and Rare in all six,
showing that its advantage on Rare holdout knowledge replicates across
both external update sources.  Mean target-update success remains
between $98.7\%$ and $100\%$ across these two benchmarks, ruling out
failed updates as the explanation for the lower Flip Rate.

\paragraph{Counterfactual specificity stress test.}
We further adapt CounterFact to the same batched-update setting.  Across
all six paired batch--seed units, Popular rehearsal reduces native
neighborhood Flip Rate from $92.8\%$ under the update-only Baseline to
$46.3\%$, compared with $66.6\%$ for Average and $65.2\%$ for Rare
rehearsal.  Popular beats each control in at least five of six paired
units, indicating a strong specificity constraint under counterfactual
updates.  Together, the three benchmarks show that Popular anchoring
consistently yields the lowest Flip Rate among the compared rehearsal
strategies, supporting its effectiveness as a general maintenance
mechanism under batched knowledge updates.
